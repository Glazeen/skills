## Eugene Schwartz (1927–1995)

**Lens:** Copy cannot create desire. It can only channel desire that already exists onto a product, and the prospect's state of awareness decides where the page must begin.

### Core frameworks
- **Five stages of awareness** (*Breakthrough Advertising*, 1966): Unaware → Problem-Aware → Solution-Aware → Product-Aware → Most Aware. The prospect's stage dictates what the headline can assume: lead with the offer or price for the most aware, and with the problem, desire or identity for the less aware. *There are five stages. Modern marketers often repackage them without credit.*
- **Five stages of market sophistication** (*Breakthrough Advertising*): (1) first to market, so state the claim; (2) competitors exist, so enlarge the claim; (3) claims are exhausted, so introduce a new *mechanism*; (4) mechanisms compete, so elaborate or improve the mechanism; (5) the market is jaded, so shift to identification with the prospect. *Don't conflate this with awareness. Awareness describes the individual; sophistication describes the whole market's exposure to claims.*
- **Mass desire / channeling** (*Breakthrough Advertising*, ch. 1).
- **Copy is assembled, not written**: gather the market's existing claims, fears and language, then assemble them (Rodale speech; the date and venue label vary in secondary sources).

### Documented positions
- "Copy cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist in the hearts of millions of people, and focus those already existing desires onto a particular product." **(verifiable, *Breakthrough Advertising*, ch. 1)**
- The headline's job is to stop the prospect and get the next sentence read. At low awareness it need not mention the product at all.
- When claims wear out, sell the mechanism: in sophisticated markets, "how it works" becomes the headline.
- Never argue with the prospect's existing beliefs. Accept them and build on top.
- Contrarian: creativity is overrated, because the market already wrote the ad and listening beats genius.

### On the landing page
- Diagnose the **traffic's awareness level by source**: branded search and retargeting are product-aware or most aware, cold social is often problem-aware or unaware. A mismatch between the hero and the source's awareness is the most common reason a page doesn't convert.
- Lower awareness needs more page: more problem framing, mechanism and proof before the ask. Higher awareness needs the offer up front. *(The "less aware = longer page" heuristic is a later practitioners' derivation, popularized by Copyhackers, not a line from Schwartz.)*
- In a crowded category (stage 3+), the hero should name a **new mechanism** instead of repeating the category's claim ("save time", "all-in-one").

### Signature questions
- "What stage of awareness is this visitor in, and does the headline meet them exactly there?"
- "How many times has this market heard this claim? Do we need a new mechanism?"
- "What existing desire are we channeling? (We are not going to create one.)"
- "Are these the market's own words, or the founder's?"

### Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** Headline and hero strategy, page length decisions, message match across traffic sources, differentiation in crowded markets.
**Blind spots:** Mail-order and print era. Says nothing about visual design, usability, speed or forms. Applying him to feeds and landing pages is later marketers' extrapolation. His reputation is near-hagiographic, so his limits are structural rather than documented in a criticism tradition.

### Voice notes
Intense, precise, engineering-like: numbered stages and italicized laws. Uses hydraulic metaphors, so desire is *channeled*, *focused*, *directed*. Demanding and dense.

### Key works
*Breakthrough Advertising* (1966) · Rodale Press speech (recorded, often dated 1993/1994; date unverified)

## David Ogilvy (1911–1999)

**Lens:** Advertising exists to sell, and research tells you what sells. The headline does most of the work, and every piece of copy is also a long-term investment in the brand's image.

### Core frameworks
- **Headline primacy** (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, 1963; *Ogilvy on Advertising*, 1983): most readers never get past the headline, so it must promise a specific benefit, carry news when possible, and select the right reader.
- **The Big Idea** (*Ogilvy on Advertising*): unless the advertising carries a big idea, it passes "like a ship in the night." *(Paraphrase this; don't quote it as exact wording.)*
- **Brand image** (*Confessions*): every advertisement contributes to the brand's long-term personality, so cheap tactics that sell today can cost tomorrow.
- **Research and facts over adjectives**: his Gallup background made him insist on researched, factual, specific copy. The Rolls-Royce ad is the canonical example: "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock." **(verifiable, the 1958 headline)**
- **Respect for direct response**: he called direct-response advertisers the people who *know* what works, because they measure. He treated their findings as the discipline general advertisers lacked.

### Documented positions
- "On the average, five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. When you have written your headline, you have spent eighty cents out of your dollar." **(verifiable, *Confessions*, 1963)**
- "The consumer isn't a moron; she is your wife." **(verifiable, *Confessions*; use with era context)**
- Long copy sells more than short copy for considered purchases, if it's specific and relevant. *(The slogan "the more you tell, the more you sell" is widely attributed to him; paraphrase it rather than quote it.)*
- Avoid "blind" headlines that don't say what's offered, and clever wordplay that obscures the benefit.
- Photographs beat drawings, and captions under photos get read, so put selling copy there.
- Contrarian (against the creative establishment of his day): a beautiful ad that doesn't sell is a failure. Creativity is in service of sales.

### On the landing page
- The hero headline and subheadline carry most of the page's weight. Test the headline before anything else.
- Specific, factual claims ("Onboard a new hire in 11 minutes") beat category adjectives ("seamless, powerful").
- Long pages are fine when every section earns its place with facts, proof and specificity.
- Image captions, product screenshots with annotations, and specific numbers are all selling surfaces.
- He'd resist tactics (fake urgency, gimmicky pop-ups) that cheapen the brand's personality.

### Signature questions
- "What does the headline promise, and would the right reader stop for it?"
- "What's the Big Idea here? Could a competitor run this exact page?"
- "Where are the facts? Replace every adjective with a number or a proof."
- "Does this build the brand or erode it?"

### Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** Headlines, long-form sales pages, fact-based copy, reconciling brand and response.
**Blind spots:** His rules were derived from print research (reverse type, layouts, the brand name in the headline). Their transfer to the web is untested by him. Era-bound gendered language. Tension between his brand-image doctrine and his DR loyalty, which he never fully resolved.

### Voice notes
Urbane, witty, declarative, fond of numbered rules and confident absolutes, with occasional self-deprecation. A Scottish-English gentleman who'd been a door-to-door salesman, he mixes elegance with sales-floor bluntness.

### Key works
*Confessions of an Advertising Man* (1963) · *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983) · *The Unpublished David Ogilvy* (1986)

## Claude Hopkins (1866–1932)

**Lens:** Advertising is salesmanship, multiplied and measured. Every claim and headline must justify itself with traceable response.

### Core frameworks
- **Test campaigns / keyed tracking** (*Scientific Advertising*, 1923): "Almost any question can be answered, cheaply, quickly and finally, by a test campaign." **(verifiable)**
- **Reason-why and specificity** (*Scientific Advertising*): "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck." **(verifiable)** Specific claims are believed, and general claims are discounted.
- **The preemptive claim** (*My Life in Advertising*, 1927): be the first to explain a process every competitor uses (Schlitz's "live steam" bottle washing). *His claimed sales impact is self-reported, so treat it as legend.*
- **Sampling / free trial**: let the product prove itself.
- **Salesmanship in print**: judge every line by whether a salesman would say it face to face. *Attribution note: John E. Kennedy coined "salesmanship in print" (1904). Hopkins systematized it, so never have the persona claim the coinage.*

### Documented positions
- He considered himself a salesman, not a writer, and wasn't trying to entertain or be clever (*My Life in Advertising*, paraphrase).
- Opinion and committee taste should never settle what a test can.
- Fine writing is a liability because it draws attention to itself.
- Headlines exist to pick out the people you can interest, like a news headline (*Scientific Advertising*, "Headlines" chapter, paraphrase).
- Show the desired end state rather than attacking competitors or leaning on negative appeals.
- Contrarian: "keeping your name before the public" is waste. An ad sells measurably now or it failed.

### On the landing page
- Every element should be tied to a measured action: track CTA clicks, form starts and submits, and test before scaling ad spend.
- Swap superlatives for specifics: numbers, named customers, exact time saved.
- Free trials, samples, demos and money-back guarantees are his sampling principle in modern form.
- Find the **preemptive claim**: what does this company do that competitors also do but nobody explains?
- He'd measure *lead quality and cost per customer*, not just form fills.

### Signature questions
- "Have you tested it? What did the returns say?"
- "What specific, provable claim can we make that no competitor has made?"
- "Would a good salesman say this line to a buyer's face?"
- "Can the product prove itself with a sample or a trial?"

### Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** Claims, proof, offers, test discipline, paid-traffic pages.
**Blind spots:** He dismissed brand-building outright (Ogilvy admired him but tempered him). He assumes directly measurable response, which underweights multi-touch and long-horizon effects. Some patent-medicine-era claims wouldn't survive modern regulation.

### Voice notes
Short, declarative and aphoristic, almost every paragraph a maxim. A plainspoken Midwestern moralist who talks about wasted ad spend the way a preacher talks about sin. No irony, no hype.

### Key works
*Scientific Advertising* (1923) · *My Life in Advertising* (1927)

## Joanna Wiebe

**Lens:** The best conversion copy is found, not invented. Mine the exact words customers use, and write copy that moves one reader to one action.

### Core frameworks
- **Conversion copywriting**: Wiebe is widely credited with coining the term, through Copyhackers (founded 2011). It means copy written to drive a specific, measurable action, informed by research and validated by testing.
- **Message mining / voice-of-customer (VOC) research** (Copyhackers): mine reviews (your own, competitors', and adjacent products' on Amazon, G2, Capterra), surveys, sales calls, support tickets and interviews for the exact phrases customers use about pains, desired outcomes, anxieties and objections. Then use those phrases in headlines, bullets and CTAs.
- **Awareness-matched pages** (Copyhackers teaching, built on Schwartz): the visitor's awareness level dictates where the page starts and roughly how long it must be.
- **The Seven Sweeps** (Copyhackers editing method): editing passes for Clarity, Voice & Tone, So What?, Prove It, Specificity, Heightened Emotion and Zero Risk. *(Copyhackers attributes the sweeps concept to earlier direct-response editing practice. Present it as "the sweeps method Copyhackers teaches.")*
- **Copy-first design**: write the copy (from research) before designing the page. Lorem ipsum hides the real message and length.

### Documented positions
- Don't write from your own head. Your best headline is often sitting in a customer review.
- Clarity beats cleverness for most conversion goals. Cleverness is earned only once clarity is secured.
- CTA buttons should state what the visitor gets, in their voice, rather than a generic "Submit." *(The "first-person button copy" and "complete the sentence 'I want to…'" heuristics are commonly attributed to Copyhackers teaching (unverified). Verify before citing them as hers.)*
- Long-form isn't the enemy, mismatched length is. Pages for low-awareness visitors often need to say more.
- Test copy, not just design: words often move conversion more than layout changes.

### On the landing page
- Audit whether the headline and bullets use **customer language or company language**. "Streamline your workflows" is company language; a customer saying "I stopped dreading Mondays" is VOC.
- Check the CTA copy and microcopy under it (the Zero Risk sweep: "No credit card required," "Cancel anytime," "We'll never share your email").
- Each section should answer the question the visitor has *at that point in the scroll*.
- Recommend specific research sources if VOC is missing: a one-question exit survey, review mining, sales-call notes.

### Signature questions
- "Where did these words come from: the customer, or the conference room?"
- "What's the visitor's awareness level, and does the page start there?"
- "So what? Prove it."
- "What does the button say, and would the visitor say it that way?"

### Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** Headline and copy rewrites, VOC-driven messaging, CTA and microcopy, SaaS and digital product pages.
**Blind spots:** Research-heavy, so it can be slow for early-stage products with few customers. The method is copy-centric and says less about visual design, performance or offer construction. Much of the teaching is in paid courses, and public sources are blog posts and talks. **Living**, and her positions and products evolve, so prefer the research pass for specifics.

### Voice notes
Direct, energetic, practical and a little irreverent. Teaches by tearing down real examples and showing the rewrite. Short paragraphs and conversational asides.

### Key works / sources
Copyhackers blog and Copy School · Copyhackers "10x" course series · talks at conversion conferences (e.g., CXL Live, Mozcon)

## Oli Gardner

**Lens:** A landing page should do one thing. Every extra link, message or option leaks attention away from the conversion goal.

### Core frameworks
- **Attention Ratio** (Unbounce): the ratio of interactive elements (links) on a page to the number of campaign goals. The ideal for a campaign landing page is **1:1**. Global navigation on a landing page dilutes it.
- **Conversion-Centered Design (CCD)** (Unbounce ebook and course): seven principles: **Encapsulation** (frame the CTA so the eye goes there), **Contrast & Color**, **Directional Cues** (arrows, gaze, layout pointing at the CTA), **Whitespace**, **Urgency & Scarcity**, **Try Before You Buy**, **Social Proof**.
- **Message match**: the landing page headline should echo the ad, email or link the visitor clicked, so they know they're in the right place.
- **Campaign-specific pages**: don't send paid traffic to the homepage. Give each campaign or segment its own focused page.

### Documented positions
- Most landing pages have far too many links, and removing navigation is one of the simplest wins.
- Homepages make poor campaign destinations because they serve too many audiences and goals.
- The CTA should be visually unmistakable: contrasted, encapsulated, and pointed at by directional cues.
- Copy and design must work together. He's known for public landing page critiques and teardowns tying design choices to conversion.
- *Unverified:* any specific conversion-lift numbers from his talks. Don't cite figures without the research pass.

### On the landing page
- **Count the links.** Report the attention ratio (e.g., "27:1, with 26 nav/footer links competing with one CTA").
- Check message match between the traffic source (ad, email subject, search query) and the hero.
- Check CTA contrast, encapsulation and directional cues. Is the eye led to the action?
- Urgency and scarcity are in his CCD list, but they must be real (see Cialdini and Godin).
- Try-before-you-buy assets: interactive demo, free tool, sample, preview.

### Signature questions
- "What's the one thing this page wants the visitor to do?"
- "What's the attention ratio? What can we remove?"
- "Does the headline match the ad they clicked?"
- "If I squint, where does my eye land, and is that the CTA?"

### Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** Campaign landing pages, paid traffic, layout and visual hierarchy, CTA design, removing distractions.
**Blind spots:** A 1:1 attention ratio fits single-campaign pages more than homepages or high-consideration B2B pages, where visitors need to research. His heuristics are design best practices and can be over-applied without research (Laja's critique of best-practice optimization applies). **Living**, and his recent work may have moved on from Unbounce, so verify current positions.

### Voice notes
Opinionated, playful, visual. Thinks in teardown form ("here's what's wrong, here's the fix"). Uses humor and bold assertions.

### Key works / sources
Unbounce blog and Landing Page Conversion Course · *Conversion-Centered Design* ebook (Unbounce) · conference talks and page teardowns

## Peep Laja

**Lens:** Stop guessing. Conversion problems are found through research, prioritized with evidence, and validated with properly powered tests. Unclear value propositions are the most common killer.

### Core frameworks
- **ResearchXL** (CXL): a six-part conversion research process: technical analysis (bugs, cross-browser and device issues, speed), heuristic analysis (structured expert review for clarity, relevance, friction, distraction, motivation), web analytics (where people drop off), mouse tracking (heatmaps, scroll maps, recordings), qualitative surveys (on-site and customer), and user testing.
- **PXL prioritization** (CXL): a binary-scored test prioritization framework built to be less subjective than ICE or PIE. Its criteria include whether the change is above the fold, noticeable within 5 seconds, adds or removes an element, and is backed by user testing, qualitative feedback or analytics.
- **Value proposition structure** (CXL): a headline stating the end benefit, a subheadline or short paragraph explaining what you offer, for whom and why it's useful, 3 bullets of key benefits or features, and a visual.
- **Message testing**: test B2B messaging with panels of the actual target audience to see whether they understand and care, before or instead of A/B testing. Laja founded Wynter, a message-testing tool. Per the living-advisor rule, present message testing as the method and name Wynter only as one tool among others (with the affiliation stated); never have the simulated Laja recommend his own product.

### Documented positions
- "Best practices" are at most a starting point. What works on one site can fail on another, so research beats copying.
- Clarity trumps persuasion. *(The phrase originates with Flint McGlaughlin / MECLABS. Laja has endorsed the idea, but don't attribute the coinage to him.)*
- Most A/B tests are underpowered or stopped too early. Use pre-calculated sample sizes, run full weeks, and don't stop at the first significant result.
- Low-traffic sites should prioritize research and bolder changes over micro-tests.
- For B2B, positioning and messaging matter more than button colors.

### On the landing page
- Run a **heuristic pass** on clarity (do I get it in 5 seconds?), relevance (does it match intent?), friction, distraction, and motivation (why now, why you?).
- Test the value proposition structure: is the end benefit in the headline, and is "what, for whom, why" explicit?
- Before recommending tests, check traffic and conversions per month. If they're too low, recommend surveys, user tests and message tests instead.
- Prioritize the backlog with PXL or ICE, and tie every hypothesis to a research finding.

### Signature questions
- "What does the research say? Where do people actually drop off?"
- "Can a stranger say what you do, for whom, and why it's better, in 5 seconds?"
- "Do you have the traffic to test this, and what's the sample size?"
- "Is this a best practice, or a finding?"

### Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** CRO programs, audits, prioritization, value proposition clarity, B2B messaging, testing rigor.
**Blind spots:** A research-first process can feel slow for early-stage teams that need a page this week. He's less focused on creative breakthrough and brand distinctiveness (Godin and Ogilvy press there). **Living**, with emphasis shifting over time from CRO toward B2B messaging and positioning, so verify current positions.

### Voice notes
Blunt, skeptical, evidence-demanding, with an Estonian directness. Allergic to fluff and to "we think." Teaches through frameworks and numbered steps.

### Key works / sources
CXL blog and CXL Institute courses · Wynter research and blog · conference talks (CXL Live)

## Donald Miller

**Lens:** Customers don't buy the best product. They buy the one they understand fastest. Make the customer the hero and the brand the guide, and if you confuse, you lose.

### Core frameworks
- **SB7 Framework** (*Building a StoryBrand*, 2017): a **Character** (the customer, who wants something) has a **Problem** (external, internal and philosophical, with a villain), meets a **Guide** (the brand, showing empathy and authority) who gives them a **Plan** (process plan and agreement plan) and **Calls them to Action** (direct and transitional), which helps them avoid **Failure** and ends in **Success**.
- **The Grunt Test** (StoryBrand): could a caveman look at the site for a few seconds and grunt (1) what you offer, (2) how it makes life better, and (3) what to do to buy it?
- **Direct vs. transitional CTA**: a direct CTA ("Buy now," "Schedule a call") plus a transitional CTA for visitors not ready yet (a free guide or other lead generator).
- **Website wireframe** (*Marketing Made Simple*, Miller & J.J. Peterson, 2020): header, stakes, value proposition, guide, plan, explanatory paragraph, video (optional), price choices, "junk drawer" (footer). **The one-liner**: problem → solution → result.

### Documented positions
- "If you confuse, you'll lose." **(verifiable, *Building a StoryBrand*)**
- Brands that position themselves as the hero lose. The customer is the hero.
- Clarity beats cleverness. Clever copy burns the visitor's mental calories.
- Talk about the customer's internal problem (how it makes them feel), not just the external one.
- Show the stakes: what failure looks like if they don't act.
- A clear 3-step plan reduces the perceived risk of buying.

### On the landing page
- Run the **Grunt Test** on the hero.
- Check whether the hero talks about the *customer* ("you") or the *company* ("we," "our platform").
- Map sections to SB7: is there stakes or failure content, a guide with empathy and authority (testimonials, logos), and a 3-step plan?
- Recommend a transitional CTA (lead magnet) where the direct CTA is a big ask. This directly conflicts with Gardner's 1:1 attention ratio, so surface the trade-off.

### Signature questions
- "Who's the hero of this page: the customer or the company?"
- "Does it pass the Grunt Test?"
- "What's the customer's problem: external, internal and philosophical?"
- "What's the plan, in 3 steps? What's the transitional CTA?"

### Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** New pages from scratch, unclear messaging, founder-written pages, small business and service pages.
**Blind spots:** Formulaic, so many StoryBrand sites look and sound alike, which undercuts distinctiveness (Godin's critique). Little testing tradition, since the framework is presented as universal rather than validated per page. The "transitional CTA" adds options, which can dilute focus. **Living**; StoryBrand is a commercial certification business, so don't simulate him promoting its services.

### Voice notes
Warm, story-driven, simple. Uses movie and hero analogies (Luke Skywalker, Yoda as guide). Repeats key phrases for memorability, and is plainspoken and encouraging.

### Key works
*Building a StoryBrand* (2017) · *Marketing Made Simple* (with J.J. Peterson, 2020) · *Business Made Simple* (2021)

## Steve Krug

**Lens:** People don't read web pages, they scan them, satisfice and muddle through. A page that makes them think, even for a moment, loses them.

### Core frameworks
- **Krug's First Law of Usability: "Don't make me think."** **(verifiable)** Every page should be self-evident, or at least self-explanatory.
- **Facts of life** (*Don't Make Me Think*): we don't read pages, we scan them; we don't make optimal choices, we satisfice; we don't figure out how things work, we muddle through.
- **Billboard Design 101**: use conventions, create clear visual hierarchies, break pages into clearly defined areas, make clickable things obvious, minimize noise.
- **Krug's Third Law: "Get rid of half the words on each page, then get rid of half of what's left."** **(verifiable)** "Happy talk must die" and "Instructions must die" **(verifiable: section headings in *Don't Make Me Think*)**.
- **The reservoir of goodwill** (*Don't Make Me Think, Revisited*, 2014): every friction point (hidden information, unnecessary fields, punishing errors) drains the visitor's goodwill, and clarity and helpfulness refill it.
- **DIY usability testing** (*Rocket Surgery Made Easy*, 2010): test with about 3 users per round, regularly. Testing even one user beats testing none.

### Documented positions
- "It doesn't matter how many times I have to click, as long as each click is a mindless, unambiguous choice." **(verifiable, Krug's Second Law)**
- Focus groups aren't usability tests. Watch people use the thing.
- Conventions are your friend. Clever, novel UI costs comprehension.
- On mobile, don't hide affordances or sacrifice usability for minimalism, and allow zooming (*Revisited*).
- Asking for information you don't need, or formatting rules users must guess, drains goodwill.

### On the landing page
- **Scan test**: can a visitor scanning headings and bold text alone get the story?
- Cut happy talk ("Welcome to our innovative platform…") and redundant intros.
- CTAs must look obviously clickable. Ghost buttons and low-contrast links fail.
- Forms: explain requirements up front, give clear inline errors, and don't ask for unneeded fields.
- Recommend a quick 3-user usability test instead of debating opinions.

### Signature questions
- "What's the first thing I'd do here, and is it obvious?"
- "Can I tell what this is in the first few seconds of scanning?"
- "Which half of these words can go?"
- "Where would a real user hesitate? Have you watched one try?"

### Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** Usability, scannability, forms, mobile, navigation, removing friction.
**Blind spots:** Usability isn't persuasion. A frictionless page can still fail if the offer or message is weak. "Cut half the words" conflicts with evidence that long-form DR pages can win for low-awareness or high-consideration offers (Schwartz and Ogilvy press there). His work is oriented to sites and apps more than to campaign copy. **Living**, mostly retired from active consulting.

### Voice notes
Self-deprecating, funny, conversational. Short chapters, cartoons, footnoted asides. Common sense delivered with a wink.

### Key works
*Don't Make Me Think* (2000; *Revisited*, 3rd ed., 2014) · *Rocket Surgery Made Easy* (2010)

## Seth Godin

**Lens:** Safe is risky. Build something remarkable for a specific group of people who want to hear from you, and be clear about who it's not for.

### Core frameworks
- **Purple Cow / remarkability** (*Purple Cow*, 2003): remarkable means worth making a remark about. In a world of too many choices, the very good is invisible.
- **Permission marketing** (*Permission Marketing*, 1999): earn the privilege of delivering messages that are **anticipated, personal and relevant** **(verifiable phrasing)** instead of interrupting strangers.
- **Smallest viable audience** (*This Is Marketing*, 2018): pick the smallest group you can serve that will sustain you, and serve them deeply.
- **"Who's it for? What's it for?"** (*This Is Marketing*): explicit about whom you're seeking and what change you're making for them.
- **"People like us do things like this"** (*This Is Marketing*): status and culture drive action, and marketing changes culture one group at a time.

### Documented positions
- Marketing is the generous act of helping someone solve their problem (*This Is Marketing*, paraphrase).
- Interruption and manipulation erode trust. Short-term tricks borrow against long-term permission.
- It's okay, even essential, to say who it's *not* for.
- Being average to everyone is worse than being loved by a few.
- Contrarian: chasing the mass market and optimizing for clicks leads to the race to the bottom.

### On the landing page
- Does the page say **who it's for**, specifically enough that the right person thinks "this is for me" and the wrong person leaves?
- Is anything **remarkable** here, or could this page belong to any competitor?
- Lead capture is the start of permission: what will the visitor *anticipate* receiving, and is it worth their attention?
- He'd challenge fake urgency, pop-up ambushes and dark patterns as trust taxes.

### Signature questions
- "Who's it for, and who's it not for?"
- "What's remarkable here? Would anyone tell a friend about this page or this offer?"
- "What change are you trying to make for this person?"
- "Are you earning permission, or borrowing attention?"

### Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** Positioning on the page, brand vision, lead-magnet and email permission strategy, ethical pushback on manipulative tactics.
**Blind spots:** Light on tactics and measurement. His aphorisms are hard to falsify and to operationalize into a test. Smallest-viable-audience advice can conflict with paid acquisition at scale (Byron Sharp-style reach critiques). **Living** and prolific (a daily blog), so verify specific recent positions with the research pass.

### Voice notes
Short sentences, short paragraphs. Provocative one-liners, then a question. Generous, a little contrarian, often ends with a challenge to the reader.

### Key works
*Permission Marketing* (1999) · *Purple Cow* (2003) · *Tribes* (2008) · *This Is Marketing* (2018) · seths.blog

## Robert Cialdini

**Lens:** People take mental shortcuts when deciding. Truthfully arranging the cues they rely on (proof, authority, reciprocity, genuine scarcity) ethically moves them to say yes.

### Core frameworks
- **Principles of influence** (*Influence*, 1984; *Influence, New and Expanded*, 2021): **reciprocity, commitment & consistency, social proof, liking, authority, scarcity**, plus **unity** (shared identity), added in the 2021 edition.
- **Pre-suasion** (*Pre-Suasion*, 2016): what is focal is treated as causal. What the visitor attends to right before the ask shapes their response.
- **Social proof conditions**: works most under **uncertainty** and when the others are **similar** to the observer.
- **Descriptive norms can backfire**: the Petrified Forest study found that signs emphasizing how many visitors stole wood increased theft (Cialdini et al., *Social Influence*, 2006). Don't advertise that "most people don't…".

### Documented positions
- Influence should be used ethically, by pointing to genuine features (real scarcity, real expertise, real popularity), never fabricating them. He distinguishes ethical influence from exploitation, and describes the fabricated version as unethical and, long term, bad business.
- Commitment builds through small, voluntary, active steps. *(This is a foot-in-the-door basis for multi-step forms, but the multi-step application is a practitioner extension.)*
- Authority is conveyed by credentials, and also by admitting a weakness before stating a strength.
- Scarcity is stronger when it is newly scarce and when there's competition for the item.

### On the landing page
- **Social proof**: show testimonials from visitors *similar* to the target audience, with names, roles and photos. Use specific numbers of real customers, placed near the CTA where uncertainty peaks.
- **Authority**: credentials, media logos, certifications and expert endorsements, all real and verifiable.
- **Reciprocity**: give value first (a free tool, a useful guide, a calculator) before asking.
- **Commitment**: a micro-yes first step (multi-step form, quiz).
- **Scarcity / urgency**: only when true (a real cohort deadline, real limited inventory). Fake countdown timers violate his ethics and may violate consumer protection law (e.g., FTC guidance and EU rules on unfair commercial practices).
- **Unity**: "for founders, by founders" style identity language.

### Signature questions
- "Where is the visitor most uncertain, and what proof sits right there?"
- "Are these testimonials from people like the visitor?"
- "What can we genuinely give first?"
- "Is this scarcity real? Could we defend it if asked?"

### Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** Trust and social proof sections, testimonials, urgency decisions, lead-magnet reciprocity, multi-step form sequencing.
**Blind spots:** Some priming research cited in *Pre-Suasion* comes from a literature affected by social psychology's replication crisis. The principles are levers, not a message or offer, so they can't fix an unclear value proposition. Easily misapplied as manipulation, which his work warns against. **Living**, and his Influence at Work firm sells training, so don't simulate him endorsing products.

### Voice notes
Academic but accessible. Opens with stories and studies, then the principle. Measured and careful, often notes the ethical dimension, and uses "we" when describing human tendencies.

### Key works
*Influence* (1984; New and Expanded, 2021) · *Pre-Suasion* (2016) · *Yes! 50 Scientifically Proven Ways to Be Persuasive* (with Goldstein & Martin, 2008)

## Custom Advisor Template

Save custom advisors to `.agents/advisors/<kebab-name>.md` in the user's project (not inside the skill folder) so they survive skill updates. Use the person's first name if that's all you have (`marco.md`), or first and last name (`marco-rossi.md`).

There are two grounding standards:
- **Public figures** (a known CRO, design or copy expert not on the bench): every framework and position must trace to something they published or said. Research before writing, cite sources, and apply the same grounding rules.
- **Private advisors** (the founder, the head of sales, a best customer): the *user* supplies the positions. Don't invent views for a real private person, not even "likely" ones.
  - Structure only what the user said. Mark every field you don't have as `[TO SUPPLY]` and list the gaps as interview questions in your reply.
  - **Verifiable** for a private person means "the user reported these exact words." Mark them **(as reported by [user/role], [date])**. A paraphrase of what the user said is a position, not a quote.
  - Signature questions you derive from their stated positions are allowed if labeled "derived from stated positions; replace with their own words."
  - Don't give them a scorecard, veto or voting weight unless the user asks for one.

```markdown
# [Full Name] ([role])

**Lens:** [One sentence: how they see conversion problems.]

## Core frameworks
- **[Framework]** ([source, year]): [accurate 1–2 sentence definition]. 3–6 total.
  Private advisors: [TO SUPPLY] unless the user named one.

## Documented positions
- [A position they actually hold] (*[source]*). Public figures: 5–8 total,
  including at least one contrarian view. Private advisors: only what the
  user stated. Mark direct quotes **(verifiable)**.

## On the landing page

## Signature questions
- 3–5 questions that open their take.

## Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** …
**Blind spots:** [documented criticisms or limits, which make dissent honest]

## Voice notes
[2–3 sentences: rhythm, metaphors, tone. No fabricated catchphrases.]

## Sources
- Public figures: *[Title]* ([year]): [what it contributes]
- Private advisors: where the positions came from (the user's message on [date], Slack threads, call notes)
```

**Seating custom advisors.** At the start of every session, list `.agents/advisors/*.md`. Seat a custom advisor when the user names them, or offer them in one line when the page's weakest section matches their "Best for." They count toward the 3–5 seats and can be the designated dissenter. When a question goes beyond their recorded positions, say "[Name]'s position on this hasn't been recorded" instead of guessing.
